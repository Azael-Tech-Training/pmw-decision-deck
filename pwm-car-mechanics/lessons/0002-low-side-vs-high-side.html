Lesson 0002 • Circuit Design

Low-Side vs. High-Side Switching

In our last lesson, we saw how the Engine Control Unit (ECU) uses transistors as high-speed switches to pulse-width modulate power to actuators. Today, we will explore where that transistor switch is placed in the circuit, how the transistor operates internally, and why this placement is critical for vehicle diagnosis and safety.

The ECU's Solid-State Relays: MOSFETs

An ECU contains microcontrollers that operate at 5V or 3.3V and can output only a few milliamperes of current. To control a radiator cooling fan drawing 15 Amperes at 12V, the ECU uses a MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) as an electronic switch. There are two primary types of MOSFETs used for this:

  • N-Channel MOSFET: Turns ON when a positive voltage is applied to its Gate relative to its Source. They are highly efficient, cheap to manufacture, and have extremely low internal resistance ($R_{DS(on)}$).
  • P-Channel MOSFET: Turns ON when the Gate voltage is pulled low relative to its Source. They are slightly less efficient and more expensive than N-channel MOSFETs, but are useful for directly controlling the power side of a circuit.

Switching Strategies: Low-Side vs. High-Side

Depending on the system's needs, automotive engineers position the transistor switch in one of two configurations:

🔌 Low-Side Switching (Standard Config)

The transistor switch is placed on the ground side of the actuator. The actuator is permanently connected to +12V battery power. To turn the device on, the ECU applies voltage to the gate of an N-channel MOSFET, closing the switch and completing the circuit path to ground.

⚡ High-Side Switching (Safety Config)

The transistor switch is placed on the power side of the actuator. The actuator is permanently connected to the metal chassis (ground). To turn the device on, the ECU switch closes, delivering +12V battery power to the control line. This is often done using P-channel MOSFETs or specialized "Smart High-Side Drivers" that include built-in current sensors.

Why Location Matters: The Short-to-Ground Fault

Cars are made of metal, and the entire chassis acts as the negative terminal (ground). Wiring harnesses run through tight metal channels where vibration can rub off wire insulation.
