ECU Control Logic and Sensor Feedback

You have hit on a critical fundamental concept: Pure Direct Current (DC) is essentially a PWM signal running at 100% duty cycle and 0 Hz frequency. Today, we will explore why this is true, how the ECU calculates what duty cycle to output, and why the switching frequency stays constant.

1. The Two Extremes: 0% and 100% Duty Cycle

A pulse-width modulated signal is defined by its switching action. But what happens at the very bottom and the very top of the power range?

  • 0% Duty Cycle: The switch is permanently open. The voltage is a flat 0V. Since there is no switching transition, there are no cycles per second, meaning the frequency is 0 Hz.
  • 100% Duty Cycle: The switch is permanently closed. The voltage is a flat 12V (or 5V). Since the signal never drops low, it never completes a cycle. The frequency is 0 Hz. This is pure, continuous DC voltage.

Between these two extremes (e.g., 1% to 99% duty cycle), the ECU actively pulses the circuit at a set **Frequency** (number of pulses per second) to regulate the average power.

2. How the ECU Calculates the Duty Cycle

The ECU does not guess. It determines the correct duty cycle dynamically using a process called **Closed-Loop Feedback Control**. Let's use a radiator cooling fan as an example:

  1. The Input (Sensor): The ECU continuously reads the **Engine Coolant Temperature (ECT)** sensor.
  2. The Target (Setpoint): The ECU has a target operating temperature programmed into its memory (e.g., 90°C).
  3. The Calculation (Error): The ECU subtracts the target temperature from the actual temperature to calculate the **Error**.
  4. The Map Lookup (Output): The ECU refers to a 2D look-up table (or "map") in its code. For example:
    • Under 85°C: Output **0% Duty Cycle** (Fan is OFF).
    • At 90°C: Output **30% Duty Cycle** (Fan runs quietly).
    • At 100°C: Output **75% Duty Cycle** (Fan runs fast).
    • Over 110°C: Output **100% Duty Cycle** (Fan runs full blast).

3. Why is Frequency Kept Constant?

While the **Duty Cycle** changes constantly to regulate fan speed, the switching **Frequency** (e.g., 150 Hz) remains **Fixed**. The ECU does not change the frequency on the fly. Why?

Because the frequency is selected by engineers to match the physical design of the actuator and driver circuit. A frequency that is too low causes the motor to vibrate and hum loudly. A frequency that is too high causes the transistor switch (MOSFET) to generate excessive heat due to switching losses. Once chosen, the frequency stays constant, and only the width of the pulse is adjusted.
