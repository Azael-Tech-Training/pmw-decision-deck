Lesson 0007 • Time Modulation

How the ECU Changes Duty Cycle

A common misconception is that the ECU changes the duty cycle by opening a switch "partway," like a water faucet or a light dimmer. In reality, a solid-state switch (MOSFET) is strictly binary: **it is either 100% ON or 100% OFF.**

So, how does the ECU command 30% power, 50% power, or 80% power? The answer is **Time**.

1. The Dimmer vs. the Speed Switch

Instead of restricting the flow of voltage (which generates massive heat and wastes energy), the ECU keeps the voltage at a full 12V but modulates **how long** the switch stays closed versus open within a tiny window of time (a cycle).

For example, let's look at a circuit operating at a frequency of 100 Hz. This means there are **100 cycles every second**. Each single cycle lasts exactly **10 milliseconds** ($100\text{ Hz} = 1\text{ second} / 100 = 10\text{ms}$):

  • To get 10% Duty Cycle: The ECU closes the switch for **1 ms** (ON), then opens it for **9 ms** (OFF). The load gets a tiny nudge of power, then coasts.
  • To get 50% Duty Cycle: The ECU closes the switch for **5 ms** (ON), then opens it for **5 ms** (OFF). The load gets power half the time.
  • To get 90% Duty Cycle: The ECU closes the switch for **9 ms** (ON), then opens it for **1 ms** (OFF). The load gets constant push with only tiny interruptions.

2. Why Doesn't the Motor Stutter?

Because the switching happens so fast (hundreds or thousands of times a second), the mechanical inertia of the fan motor and the electrical inductance of its copper coils prevent it from stopping during the "OFF" times. The motor doesn't have time to slow down; it simply averages out the pulses and spins smoothly at a steady speed.

Summary: The Power is in the Timing

The ECU changes duty cycle **not** by changing the height of the voltage steps (which always pulse to 12V), but by changing how long the voltage stays high vs. low in each cycle. By modulating time, the ECU controls average power with almost zero energy waste.
